Security Analysis of Microsoft 365 Encryption System
Overview of Microsoft 365 Encryption Mechanisms
Microsoft 365 employs a multi-layered encryption strategy to protect the confidentiality and integrity of user data (Microsoft, 2023). At the data-at-rest level, Microsoft 365 uses BitLocker for full-disk encryption on its servers and implements per-file encryption for user content stored in services like OneDrive and SharePoint. In OneDrive and SharePoint, each file is split into one or more chunks, and each chunk is encrypted with a unique AES-256 key; furthermore, every update (delta) to a file is encrypted with a new key specific to that chunk of data (Microsoft, 2025a). The encryption keys for these content chunks are stored separately from the encrypted data itself, in a distinct key storage service that is physically isolated from the content storage (Microsoft, 2025a). This design means that even if an attacker obtains the encrypted data from storage, they cannot decrypt the files without also compromising the keys and the file index metadata stored elsewhere, which greatly enhances security for data at rest (Microsoft, 2025a).
For data in transit, Microsoft 365 relies on industry-standard Transport Layer Security (TLS) to encrypt data as it travels between user devices and Microsoft’s cloud services (Microsoft, 2023). All client-server communications with Microsoft 365 occur over TLS/SSL channels using strong cipher suites and at least 2048-bit keys for the handshake and key exchange processes (Microsoft, 2025a). In addition, data transfers between Microsoft’s own datacenters (for example, replication of data for redundancy or backup) are conducted over private dedicated networks and are further protected with robust encryption, even though those channels are not public, to guard against eavesdropping in transit (Microsoft, 2025a). Specific applications enforce additional in-transit encryption measures; for instance, Microsoft Teams secures its messaging and calling traffic by using TLS (and mutual TLS for server authentication) for signaling, and by encrypting media streams with Secure Real-Time Transport Protocol (SRTP). Teams also uses Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS) 140-2 compliant algorithms for key exchange and encryption, ensuring that communications meet stringent government security standards (Microsoft, 2023).
At the application layer, Microsoft 365 incorporates various cryptographic techniques to protect content such as emails and documents. A cornerstone of this is the Azure Rights Management service (Azure RMS), part of Azure Information Protection, which combines symmetric and asymmetric cryptography to enforce fine-grained access control on Office documents and emails (Microsoft, 2024). In essence, whenever a document or email is protected by Azure RMS, a random symmetric key (typically AES 128-bit, known as the content key) is generated to encrypt the content. This content key is then encrypted with the organization’s 2048-bit RSA public key (the tenant key) and embedded into the document’s policy that travels with the file. Simultaneously, the author of the document or the sender of the email digitally signs the policy (using their private key), ensuring the integrity of the protected content and the authenticity of its origin (Microsoft, 2024). Each Office 365 tenant has a unique RSA key pair as its root encryption key; by default Microsoft manages these keys in its secure hardware security modules (HSMs), but customers have the option to Bring Your Own Key (BYOK) – generating and controlling their tenant key via on-premises HSMs, which is then transferred into Microsoft’s HSMs under the customer’s control (Microsoft, 2024). This design choice strengthens protection against insider threats: even Microsoft administrators cannot freely access customer-managed keys or decrypt content without authorization, since such keys remain non-exportable and all access to them in the cloud is logged and audited (Microsoft, 2024).
Microsoft 365 offers multiple options for email encryption to accommodate different scenarios. Office 365 Message Encryption (OME), for example, is built on Azure Information Protection and allows users to send encrypted, rights-protected emails to any recipient (even outside the organization) (Microsoft, 2024). OME leverages the Azure RMS infrastructure to protect emails from interception and man-in-the-middle attacks during transit, and it can enforce usage restrictions on an email (such as preventing forwarding or printing) if coupled with additional Information Rights Management policies (Mailprotector, 2021). Another option is S/MIME (Secure/Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions), which is supported in Exchange Online for organizations that use certificate-based end-to-end email encryption and digital signatures (Microsoft, 2024). With S/MIME, the email content is encrypted using the recipient’s public key and can only be decrypted by the recipient’s corresponding private key, meaning that even Microsoft’s servers cannot read the content of S/MIME-encrypted emails (Microsoft, 2024). In practice, organizations using S/MIME distribute users’ public key certificates to a directory (such as Azure Active Directory) for lookup, while the private keys remain solely in the users’ possession (often stored on their local devices or smart cards) (Microsoft, 2024). This ensures that only the intended recipient can access the message, though it also implies trade-offs in terms of malware scanning and compliance, since the service cannot inspect or filter S/MIME-encrypted messages without access to the keys (Mailprotector, 2021).
In summary, the Microsoft 365 encryption architecture employs a combination of symmetric encryption algorithms (primarily AES), asymmetric encryption (RSA 2048 and increasingly elliptic-curve algorithms for TLS key exchange), cryptographic hashing (e.g. SHA-256), and digital signature techniques to secure data at rest and in transit across the service (Microsoft, 2024; Microsoft, 2025a). Microsoft regularly updates the supported encryption standards, phasing out deprecated or weak algorithms in favor of current, rigorously vetted cipher suites, in line with or exceeding industry and government security standards such as FIPS 140-2 (Microsoft, 2025b). For example, in late 2023 Microsoft began updating the default encryption mode for Office documents and emails to use AES-256 in Cipher Block Chaining (CBC) mode, replacing the earlier AES-128 in ECB mode, in order to provide stronger protection (Microsoft, 2023). Together, these measures form a comprehensive encryption strategy within Microsoft 365, aimed at mitigating the risk of unauthorized data access or interception.
Cryptography Research and Industry in Australia: Achievements and Challenges
Compared to regions like the US and Europe, Australia’s footprint in cryptographic research and development has been relatively modest, but there have been notable achievements and focused efforts in recent years. One area of strength is 
post-quantum cryptography (PQC)
 research, which prepares for future threats from quantum computers. Australian researchers have been active in global collaborations to develop new quantum-resistant encryption algorithms that could safeguard current encryption protocols from quantum attacks (CSIRO, 2023). For instance, a team led by Monash University in partnership with the national science agency CSIRO’s Data61 unit developed a highly efficient quantum-secure cryptographic algorithm called “LaV,” intended to enhance the security of end-to-end encryption against quantum adversaries (CSIRO, 2023). This algorithm has been reported as one of the most efficient quantum-safe encryption methods to date and was presented at the Crypto 2023 international cryptology conference, reflecting international recognition of the work (CSIRO, 2023). Such accomplishments highlight that Australian academics are contributing to the 
forefront of cryptography research, addressing emerging security challenges like the post-quantum threat.
Several Australian universities and research institutes maintain active groups in cryptography and related fields. The University of Wollongong, for example, hosts an Institute of Cybersecurity and Cryptology that conducts research into cryptographic algorithms, protocols, and cybersecurity practices. Monash University and the University of New South Wales (UNSW) also have faculty known for work in public-key cryptography and provable security (notably, researchers like Associate Professor Ron Steinfeld at Monash, who is an expert in lattice-based and quantum-safe cryptographic algorithms, and collaborators at UNSW engaged in cryptographic protocol research) (CSIRO, 2023). Moreover, CSIRO’s Data61, often in cooperation with university partners, has driven applied cryptography projects in areas such as secure multiparty computation, privacy-preserving data sharing, and blockchain security. Australian teams have also participated in international cryptography initiatives—for instance, contributing submissions or analysis to the NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography standardization project. These collaborative efforts ensure that Australia’s expertise is present in the global conversation about next-generation cryptographic standards, even if Australian proposals must compete with those from larger research communities worldwide.
Despite these positives, there are significant challenges and gaps facing the Australian cryptography sector. Firstly, in terms of industry and commercialization, Australia has produced relatively few globally recognized cryptographic products or algorithms. Many government agencies and companies in Australia rely on established foreign cryptographic standards and implementations. For example, ubiquitous algorithms like AES and RSA were developed outside Australia and adopted as international standards; Australia has not yet seen a home-grown algorithm achieve similar worldwide adoption. This reliance on foreign technology implies that domestic innovation in foundational cryptography is limited. There are only a handful of Australian companies specializing in cryptographic solutions; a notable example is 
Senetas
, a company known for developing high-performance network encryption hardware. Senetas produces certified high-speed encryptors used by governments, defense organizations, and enterprises in over 60 countries, and it has positioned itself as a provider of quantum-resistant encryption capabilities in its products by rapidly incorporating newly standardized post-quantum algorithms (Senetas, 2024). This is one of the few instances of Australian-made cryptographic technology with global reach. Apart from such exceptions, most Australian businesses delivering security solutions integrate internationally developed cryptographic libraries and hardware, rather than inventing their own. The small scale of the local cybersecurity industry in the encryption niche means there is less commercial drive to innovate purely within Australia, creating a dependence on global crypto research and products. This cautious adoption of cutting-edge cryptography is further evidenced by a recent industry survey: 61% of Australian organizations acknowledged the threat of quantum computing to current encryption, but only about 8.5% had begun migrating to quantum-resistant solutions (Florian, 2025).
Secondly, Australia’s talent pool and research ecosystem in cryptography, while competent, are relatively small, which can limit the output and impact of its research. Cryptography is a highly specialized field requiring strong backgrounds in mathematics and computer science. Compared to the large teams found in North America, Europe, or East Asia, Australian research groups have fewer researchers and graduate students focused on cryptography, potentially limiting the 
breadth of research topics and the frequency of breakthrough results. This challenge is somewhat self-reinforcing: limited funding and industrial demand can make it difficult to attract or retain top cryptography talent in academia, and a smaller research community means fewer opportunities for domestic collaboration and mentorship of the next generation. In terms of funding, although the Australian government and institutions have begun to invest more in cybersecurity and quantum technology research in recent years, the overall investment in cryptographic R&D has historically been lower than in regions where major cryptographic advancements originated. By contrast, the US government (through agencies like NIST and the NSA) and the EU (through academic networks and grants) have heavily funded cryptographic research and competitions for decades. Australia is catching up – for instance, by funding projects in quantum computing and sponsoring applied cryptography research through national grants – but the consistency and scale of such support may need to increase for Australia to reach a leadership position in the field.
Another factor is the policy and legal environment, which has been a double-edged sword for cryptography in Australia. On one hand, Australia, as part of the Five Eyes intelligence alliance, has a strong emphasis on signals intelligence and law enforcement access, which has led to legislation that is sometimes viewed as unfriendly to the widespread use of strong encryption. A notable example is the Telecommunications and Other Legislation Amendment (Assistance and Access) Act 2018, often referred to as the “anti-encryption law” by critics, which empowers law enforcement to compel tech companies to assist in decrypting communications under certain conditions. Such laws have raised concerns in the tech community about undermining trust in secure communication tools and could indirectly dampen enthusiasm for implementing end-to-end encryption. Additionally, Australia’s stringent export control regulations historically treated encryption technologies as dual-use goods subject to tight controls. In 2015, Australian cryptographers – including Dr. Vanessa Teague and colleagues – publicly voiced concern that the Defence Trade Controls Act could impede academic freedom by requiring permits for the “intangible” export (sharing) of cryptography research results (Nogrady, 2016). They petitioned the government to relax these restrictions to ensure that benign academic and open-source cryptography work would not be stifled by over-broad regulations (Australian Parliament, 2015). While amendments to the law have since been made to address these issues, the episode highlights a tension between national security policy and the freedom to conduct cryptographic research. Navigating such an environment can be challenging for researchers and companies alike: academics must be mindful of legal constraints when collaborating internationally, and companies might be hesitant to develop or deploy encryption features that could conflict with future regulatory requirements.
In summary, Australia’s cryptography landscape shows a mix of promising research efforts and systemic challenges. There are clear strengths in niche areas like post-quantum cryptography research and a few successful security product companies, and Australian experts are contributing to international progress in cryptography. However, the country currently lags top-tier nations in terms of cryptographic innovation infrastructure, industry capacity, and perhaps strategic prioritization. Bridging this gap will likely require sustained investment in research and education, nurturing of specialized talent, encouragement of homegrown security start-ups (perhaps through government incentives or procurement), and a supportive policy environment that recognizes robust encryption as an asset to cybersecurity rather than solely as a hurdle for 
law enforcement.
Analysis of Design Goals in Microsoft 365’s Cryptosystem
When designing or evaluating a real-world cryptosystem, security engineers typically consider core goals such as 
confidentiality
, 
integrity
, 
availability
, 
authenticity
, and 
non-repudiation
. Microsoft 365’s encryption design reflects an attempt to achieve these goals, balancing them against the practical requirements of a cloud service. Analyzing how Microsoft 365 meets these design goals provides insight into both the strengths and limitations of its security architecture.
Confidentiality
 is paramount for user data in Microsoft 365, and the service implements strong measures to ensure that data is only accessible to authorized parties. As described earlier, multiple layers of encryption protect data at rest and in transit. This means that even if data is intercepted or accessed without authorization, it remains unintelligible to the attacker. For example, TLS encryption uses robust symmetric ciphers (such as AES) to protect data in transit, with keys negotiated via asymmetric cryptography (RSA or Elliptic Curve Diffie–Hellman) so that only the communicating endpoints possess the session keys. Similarly, at rest, Microsoft 365 uses AES-256 encryption for storage, in many cases on a per-file (or per-chunk) basis (Microsoft, 2025a), ensuring that compromising a storage server yields no plaintext. Moreover, by separating where content, encryption keys, and reassembly metadata are stored, Microsoft’s design adds architectural confidentiality: an attacker would need to breach multiple independent systems to fully recover protected data (Microsoft, 2025a). This compartmentalization goes beyond relying on encryption algorithms alone; it structurally limits the damage that any single point of failure can inflict on data confidentiality.
Integrity
 and 
authenticity
 of data are maintained through cryptographic hashes and digital signatures. Microsoft 365 uses these mechanisms to detect tampering and confirm the source of data. For instance, when Azure RMS protects a document or email, it not only encrypts the content but also requires the publisher’s digital signature on the policy that accompanies the content (Microsoft, 2024). This digital signature (often using RSA or a similar algorithm) binds the identity of the content creator to the encrypted file and ensures that the usage policy or permissions have not been altered by an unauthorized party. If an adversary attempted to modify an encrypted file or its access policy, the signature verification would fail, alerting the system or the recipient that the content may have been tampered with. In data transit scenarios, integrity is provided by TLS protocols using message authentication codes (e.g., HMAC) or authenticated encryption modes; any modification of data in transit will be detected because the cryptographic checksum will not match at the receiver’s end. Additionally, authenticity of servers is ensured via TLS certificate validation (clients verify that they are connected to a legitimate Microsoft server, not an impostor). In the email context, the S/MIME option provides both encryption and digital signing. A user can sign an email with their private key, allowing recipients to verify that the email truly originated from the purported sender and was not altered en route (Microsoft, 2024). These cryptographic assurances complement confidentiality: while encryption hides information from unauthorized viewers, hashing and signing verify that information hasn’t been maliciously changed and confirm it is coming from a legitimate source.
Availability
 is another crucial consideration, even if it is less directly tied to cryptography. Encryption can sometimes conflict with availability because losing access to encryption keys might render data irretrievable to even authorized users. Microsoft 365’s design attempts to avoid encryption-related loss of availability. On the performance side, the system leverages efficient 
implementations and hardware acceleration so that encryption and decryption overhead is minimal and does not noticeably degrade the user experience. On the data recovery side, Microsoft has put in place key management procedures to prevent permanent data loss. For example, customer-managed keys in Azure (for scenarios like Customer Key) are stored in redundant HSMs, and customers are responsible for securely backing up those keys. Meanwhile, Microsoft’s service-managed keys are also redundantly stored and periodically backed up to ensure that data can be decrypted when needed (Microsoft, 2025b). Features like versioning and recycle bins in SharePoint and OneDrive further ensure that even if data becomes temporarily inaccessible (or is encrypted by ransomware, for instance), it can be restored to a previous intact version. In cases where a user leaves an organization or loses the ability to access encrypted content, Microsoft 365 provides enterprise administrators with controlled mechanisms (such as eDiscovery tools or a data recovery agent capability) to regain access to encrypted data. These mechanisms are tightly governed and audited but are essential to ensure that encryption does not unintentionally lock out legitimate stakeholders. Overall, the approach is to balance strong encryption with practical means for data owners to recover access, so that security measures do not come at the expense of the organization’s ability to use its information.
Microsoft 365’s encryption approach also reflects a balance between strict security and 
usability/business needs
 – a critical aspect of any security design. Pushing security to its theoretical maximum (for instance, using end-to-end encryption everywhere with user-held keys) could achieve confidentiality and authenticity goals in a more absolute sense, but it would also severely limit functionality such as search indexing, spam filtering, or data loss prevention, and it would complicate the user experience. Recognizing this, Microsoft 365 opts for a model where encryption is robust but the cloud service can still access data under certain controlled circumstances. Most encryption in Microsoft 365 is therefore service-mediated rather than strictly end-to-end. By default, Microsoft manages the encryption keys for data stored in its cloud, allowing the service to decrypt and inspect content for malware, to perform indexing for search, or to comply with legal disclosure requests when presented with valid warrants. From a design standpoint, this means customers are placing a degree of trust in Microsoft’s internal security controls and governance. Microsoft earns this trust by implementing strong internal safeguards (such as multi-factor authentication for its administrators, just-in-time privilege elevation, and extensive monitoring) and by undergoing third-party audits and certifications to verify its controls. For customers with higher security or compliance needs, the service offers options like Customer Key (where the customer provides an additional layer of encryption with their own keys) or even Double Key Encryption (where the customer holds one of the two keys required to decrypt data, so Microsoft alone can never access the content). These options allow organizations to opt into stricter trust models at the cost of some convenience (Microsoft, 2024). Usability is addressed by making encryption as transparent as possible to end users: most encryption is enabled by default and operates behind the scenes. Users do not typically need to manually encrypt documents or emails; they simply store data in SharePoint or send emails in Outlook, and encryption is applied automatically. By making secure practices the path of least resistance, Microsoft 365’s design reduces the likelihood of user error undermining security.
Despite its comprehensive design, a critical analysis from a security engineering perspective must consider the 
limitations and potential risks
 of the Microsoft 365 cryptosystem. One fundamental principle is that a system’s security is often only as strong as its weakest link. In the 
case of Microsoft 365, the cryptographic algorithms employed (AES, RSA, SHA-256, etc.) are robust, and there are no known feasible attacks against them when properly implemented. Therefore, attackers tend to target other vulnerabilities in the system or its users. For example, if an attacker can obtain a user’s credentials through phishing or social engineering, they can log in as that user and access data within Microsoft 365 with the same privileges, completely bypassing encryption. Similarly, if malware infects a user’s device, it could wait until the user opens an encrypted document and then steal the plaintext (by reading it directly from the screen or memory), thereby circumventing all encryption on the storage and transport. These scenarios underscore that encryption, while vital, is not a standalone solution; it must be combined with strong authentication, endpoint security, and user education to form a defense-in-depth strategy. Microsoft’s own documentation emphasizes that encryption is just one part of a larger information protection strategy and that content is still vulnerable if an authorized user’s account or device is compromised (Microsoft, 2023). In other words, the effectiveness of Microsoft 365’s encryption can be nullified by attacks that target human or software weaknesses, which is why complementary security controls are so important.
Another consideration is the 
trust model and compliance requirements
 inherent in Microsoft 365’s design. As noted, Microsoft 365 (in its default configuration) is not an end-to-end encrypted system in which the provider has zero access to data. Instead, it is designed to balance customer privacy with functionality and legal compliance. Customers must trust Microsoft to some degree to uphold the confidentiality of their data and resist any unlawful or unethical access. This trust is not blind; it is reinforced by contractual commitments, technical barriers (like the aforementioned key segregation and audits), and legal protections. However, some organizations or threat models might consider this insufficient. For instance, an enterprise worried about insider threats at the cloud provider or government surveillance may prefer to use Customer Key or client-side encryption to ensure that Microsoft cannot access their data even if it wanted to. The availability of these features shows that Microsoft 365’s cryptosystem is adaptable to different risk appetites. Yet adopting them introduces new responsibilities for the customer (such as managing keys securely) and may limit certain cloud functionalities. From a security engineering perspective, this highlights the perpetual trade-off between control and convenience. It also raises the issue of 
lawful access
: Microsoft, like other global cloud providers, has to navigate law enforcement requests and national policies. The encryption model in Microsoft 365 seeks to allow compliance with lawful orders (through Microsoft holding keys), while also giving customers tools to lock down their data if they choose. This dual approach has been the subject of debate, as some argue any accessible encryption is a potential weakness, whereas others accept it as a pragmatic necessity for complex cloud ecosystems.
Reflecting on Microsoft 365’s encryption design and the process of analyzing it, we can appreciate why this project was challenging and what insights it provided. The system’s scale and sophistication required piecing together knowledge of fundamental cryptographic building blocks and seeing how they are deployed in concert in a real service. We had to extend beyond what is obvious from documentation to critically evaluate 
why
 certain decisions might have been made – for example, why not implement everything as end-to-end encryption, or what are the implications of using a particular algorithm or key management scheme. In doing so, we considered not just the technical merits but also operational, usability, and legal aspects, mirroring the multi-faceted thinking that professional security engineers must employ. We 
encountered the interplay between theory and practice: cryptographic algorithms give us powerful tools, but achieving security in practice involves careful handling of keys, integration with user identity, and anticipating how systems can fail or be attacked. This analysis reinforced the importance of 
threat modeling
 (identifying how an adversary might try to defeat encryption and checking if the system’s design addresses those threats) and 
critical thinking
 (recognizing that every design has limitations and being able to articulate them). It was also an opportunity to reflect on our own learning – seeing abstract concepts like “defense in depth” or “principle of least privilege” manifested in a concrete system. As a result of this project, we have gained a deeper understanding of what it means to apply cryptography in a complex environment and of the careful engineering trade-offs that must be navigated. We also learned to back up our analysis with evidence from research and documentation, improving our skills in technical inquiry and professional writing. Overall, studying Microsoft 365’s cryptosystem in depth has not only expanded our knowledge of cloud security design, but also prepared us to approach future security problems with a more holistic and critically engaged mindset.
