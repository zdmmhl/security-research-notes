Abstract

In this project, the overall encryption mechanism of Microsoft 365 cloud service is studied in detail and analyzed each part of the system using the Extended CIA Triad model. At the same time, the development status and trend of Australian cryptography industry are studied and compared with the development of other countries or regions in the world. Firstly, the project outlines how Microsoft 365 protects data security through multi-level encryption technologies. Introducing the main encryption algorithms and key management schemes, as well as user authentication and access control methods. An application example of Microsoft Outlook is given to illustrate how these encryption mechanisms work in the actual office scene. Secondly, the project analyzes the development status of cryptography related industries in Australia, highlighting the problems faced by this industry in Australia. The project finds that Microsoft has built a perfect encryption and information protection system for Microsoft 365 to ensure the security of the user’s data. By analyzing from Microsoft 365 Encryption System, it is easy to see that a complete or run the encryption system may require extremely strong capabilities to be realized. However, it seems that the cryptography industry in Australia is not well-equipped to handle this task, it is extremely dependent on mature technologies from abroad. Although there are many challenges in Australia's cryptography industry, the Australian cryptography industry is trying to strive to develop under the promotion of the government and market demand, and promising results have been achieved in emerging directions.

An overview of the encryption mechanism of Microsoft 365 Microsoft 365 cloud services adopt the security principle of "data encryption in both transit and at rest" to provide users with comprehensive data protection. This means that data is strongly encrypted to prevent unauthorized access, whether it is transferred from a client device to a cloud server or stored in a Microsoft data center. At the transport layer, the service uses Transport Layer Security (TLS) protocol by default to encrypt communication sessions between clients and cloud servers, as well as communication connections between servers. It can efficiently prevent network eavesdropping and man-in-the-middle attacks. For example, when a user sends an email in Outlook, Exchange Online attempts to establish a TLS encrypted connection with the target mail server, ensuring that the message is always delivered in ciphertext route. At the storage layer, Microsoft 365 uses disk encryption and file-level encryption for data at rest: Files on SharePoint Online and OneDrive are protected by BitLocker drive encryption, and email data on Exchange Online is also stored in encrypted form. In addition, Microsoft uses technologies such as Distributed Key management (DKM) and server-side encryption to provide additional protection for cloud storage and allows customers to use their own Customer keys to increase control over encryption keys. It is worth noting that Microsoft 365 offers Office Message Encryption (now incorporated into Microsoft Purview), which is built on top of the Azure Rights Management service. It encrypts the content of email bodies and attachments, enabling organizations to send encrypted emails to recipients inside and outside the organization, regardless of their email service. If the recipient is a Microsoft or Azure AD account user, he/she can read the encrypted email directly through login verification; Other recipients can gain access to read the email, such as through a one-time verification code(Microsoft, 2023).

Through these multi-level measures, data is encrypted at all stages of transmission and storage. It highly improves the confidentiality of the whole system.

Encryption algorithms and key management used

In Microsoft 365, a wide range of industry-recognized strong encryption algorithms and protocols are used to protect data security. Transport layer security mainly relies on TLS/SSL protocols and IPSec protocols, which use asymmetric key exchange (such as RSA or elliptic Curve Diffie- Hellman) combined with symmetric encryption (such as AES) to protect confidentiality communication. In terms of data storage at rest and content encryption, Microsoft 365 makes heavy use of the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES). Starting in the second half of 2023, Microsoft Purview Information Protection has adopted AES encryption algorithm with 256-bit key length (AES-256) by default to increase security strength. As of October 2023, AES-256-CBC mode has become the default standard for encryption of document and email content in Microsoft 365 applications. This reflects Microsoft's response to the latest encryption strength requirements and ensures that user data meets strict security compliance requirements (Microsoft, 2024). On key management part, Microsoft 365 takes a complex multi-layered approach: encryption keys required by the platform by default are managed by Microsoft, and key storage and use are under strict security controls (e.g., using the hardware Security Module (HSM) to save keys). Taking Office Email Encryption (OME) as an example, the key used for email content encryption is securely managed and stored by Microsoft, and the sender and administrator do not need to distribute the key by themselves. Microsoft also offers a customer-managed master Key option, called "Customer Key," which allows organizations to host their own master key in Azure Key Vault, which is used to add an additional layer of encryption protection to their data encryption keys in Microsoft 365. In this "two-tier encryption" model, even Microsoft, as a service provider, cannot decrypt customer data without authorization, fulfilling the requirements of strict compliance environments such as finance and government that require customers to control the encryption key (Microsoft, 2022a).

Another key mechanism is Information Rights Management (IRM): Azure RMS (the IRM service behind it) generates a random content key for each protected document or email, and then encrypts the content key with a tenant-level master key (typically generated by an algorithm such as RSA 2048-bit). Only after the user has been authenticated and authorized by Azure RMS will the system issue the temporary key to the client to decrypt the content. Therefore, IRM is essentially a "content encryption + authentication + permission policy" scheme (Microsoft, 2022b). Upon a user’s access attempt, Azure Active Directory (AAD) authenticates the identity, and Azure RMS checks the assigned permissions and policies. The system will release the decrypted content key to enable access only with user’s authorization.

As the figure 3 show, this mechanism tightly combines encryption, identity, and permission policies. Furthermore, Microsoft also leverages cryptographic hash functions and digital signatures to maintain integrity and source authenticity. For instance, Azure RMS digitally signs the attached permission policy to prevent tampering, while TLS uses HMAC for data integrity in transit. S/MIME uses digital signatures to validate the sender’s identity and message integrity.

To sum up, Microsoft 365 comprehensively uses AES, RSA, TLS and other mature algorithms, and through the flexible key management mechanism of the combination of Microsoft managed key and customer self-managed key, it not only ensures the strength of data encryption, but also meets the different needs of different customers for key control. In addition, Microsoft 365 also uses cryptographic hash functions and digital signatures and other technologies to maintain data integrity and source trust. For example, when Azure RMS protects a document or email, the system not only encrypts the content, but also requires the publisher to digitally sign the attached permission policy to ensure that the permissions have not been tampered with and bind the identity of the content owner. Similarly, TLS relies on verification mechanisms such as HMAC to provide data integrity in transit, while S/MIME uses the sender's private key to sign the content of the email to ensure that the recipient can verify the integrity and authenticity of the email.

User authentication and access control

Strong encryption also requires tight authentication and access control to ensure that only authorized users have access to sensitive content. In Microsoft 365, Azure Active Directory (AAD, now Microsoft Entra ID) plays a core role in unified authentication and access management. All users, including internal enterprise employees and external guests, are authenticated and token-authorized by AAD when they access protected files or mail. The principle of Azure RMS (IRM feature) combines encryption, identity, and permission policy: When a user attempts to access an IRM-protected item, Azure RMS verifies their identity and permission via AAD before issuing a temporary decryption key. This ensures that even if encrypted content is leaked, unauthorized users cannot access it.

[Original figure omitted from the text edition.]

Except AAD, Microsoft 365 also supports S/MIME for organizations that have deployed digital certificates. Emails are encrypted using the recipient's public key and signed with the sender's private key, enabling end-to-end confidentiality and identity verification within supported environments. Microsoft 365 also enforces protection through Data Loss Prevention (DLP) policies. These can detect predefined types of sensitive data in emails and documents and automatically apply encryption or issue warnings based on organizational rules. Combined with IRM, DLP all these parts provides automated, identity-based protection against data leakage.

As the core mail client of Microsoft 365 suite, Microsoft Outlook intuitively embodies the application scenarios of the above encryption and authority control mechanisms in daily office. First, in internal communication, users can easily set permission policies such as "Do Not Forward." This invokes Azure RMS's IRM technology to encrypt the message and restrict actions like forwarding, copying, or printing. Administrators can also configure backend rules to enforce such protections automatically. Second, for cross-organizational communications, Outlook supports Office Message Encryption (OME). When a user selects "Encrypt" for an external recipient, the message is converted into an encrypted HTML attachment. The user will be guided to a secure portal (like a temporary dialog box), where both hands users can communicate safely. This method also verifies their identities through a Microsoft login or one-time code to view the content. By using this portal, it is easy to use for both users, and the reliability is well guaranteed. Third, Outlook supports S/MIME integration for organizations who have deployed user certificates. If the recipient’s certificate is available, Outlook will prompt the sender to encrypt and sign the message. Once the message is received, the client will automatically decrypt and verify the content using the local private key. Outlook will enhance security through data loss prevention (DLP) policies in the end. When sensitive information is detected during email composition, Outlook can issue warnings or automatically apply encryption based on organizational policies, preventing the message from being sent if necessary. These examples demonstrate how Microsoft 365 integrates multiple encryption strategies into a unified and accessible workflow within Outlook, supporting both compliance and usability.

Balance and analysis of security and usability

It is important to point out that Microsoft 365's encryption system, while providing strong protection, also considers the balance between usability and functional requirements, which is one of the key considerations in the design of cloud services. Instead of full end-to-end encryption, much of Microsoft 365's encryption is "service-coordinated encryption" -- by default, the cloud provider (Microsoft) holds the encryption keys to process the necessary data, subject to strict rights controls and audits. For example, to perform malware scanning, content indexing, and compliance checks. This design means that customers need to trust Microsoft, and the regulation it is subject to, to a certain extent, not to abuse access rights. To this end, Microsoft builds customer trust in its cloud services through strict internal security controls (such as enabling multi-factor authentication for administrators, Just-In-Time temporary permissions, full monitoring audits, etc.) as well as third-party compliance certifications.

For customers with more security requirements, Microsoft 365 also offers options to strengthen the separation of trust, such as Customer Key, which allows customers to manage their own keys, and even Double Key Encryption. Making it impossible for even Microsoft to decrypt data alone. These mechanisms represent a trade-off between usability and security control: user convenience and functionality are guaranteed by default, while organizations with higher security preferences are allowed to trade convenience for greater data autonomy. In addition, from a security engineering perspective, the overall protection of Microsoft 365 relies on the security of all elements of the system, not just the cryptographic algorithm itself. AES, RSA, SHA-256 and other algorithms adopted by Microsoft are widely recognized as safe in the industry, and there is no known feasible cracking method. 

However, if attackers bypass encryption and directly target system or user weaknesses, such as obtaining legitimate user account credentials through phishing, they can impersonate a user to log in to Microsoft 365 and use their rights to read data, no matter how strong the storage and transmission encryption is. Similarly, if the user's terminal is infected with malware, even if the data in the cloud is encrypted, the malware can steal the plaintext when the user decrypted the content, thus bypassing all transmission and storage encryption. These cases show that encryption is the cornerstone of confidentiality, but it is not a general solution. As for enhancing confidentiality, it must be complemented with other powerful methods. For example: strong identity authentication, terminal security and anti-phishing training, which can form a defense in depth. Microsoft's documentation also emphasizes that encryption is only one part of an overall information protection strategy: if an authorized user account or device is compromised, encrypted content can still be exposed (Microsoft, 2024). 

This architectural philosophy appeals that cryptography is embedded not as an isolated solution but as one element within a tightly, integrated security system. Instead of depending solely on cryptographic strength, Microsoft 365 distributes these security risks by combining technology, clear procedures, and analyzing user behavior. This also recognizes one of the famous theories called “Buckets effect”, which means real-world threats often bypass cryptographic protections by targeting the weakest points in the system. Look through it another way, encryption works not only as a shield but also as a tool that makes secure usage possible. It can protect data during storage and transmission while still supporting compliance, productivity, and usability in a proper condition. This shows that it's possible to have both strong security and smooth user experience, with the right design choices.

Overall, the Microsoft 365 encryption system can be called a powerful and complex one. It is not hard to see that all these Extended CIA Triad principles are well shown by different parts.

| Extended CIA Principles | Implementation in Microsoft 365 |

The cryptography industry in Australia

The previous parts of this project have shown that Microsoft 365 relies on a highly complex and deeply integrated encryption system. Its design combines advanced algorithms, complicated and numerous protocols, different settled schemes for facing different conditions.  Consequently, implementing and operating such a system requires not only technical excellence, but also a mature industrial ecosystem, experienced leadership and a bunch of professional talents.

This raises a critical question which is confusing me for years since I came to Australia: to what extent can Australia’s locals independently build and sustain encryption systems of similar scale and complexity? The answer lies not only in technical capability, but also in the state of the whole local cryptography industry. The following sections shift focus to Australia’s cryptography sector, evaluating its talent pipeline, research strength, industry details and international status. There is also a comparison highlights the gap between global best practices and local capacity, which provides some insights into Australia’s ongoing efforts to address its structural limitations.

A survey of the state of the art of cryptography in Australia

Australia's cryptography industry is an important part of the field of network security. In recent years, the Australian government and industry have paid increasing attention to the capacity building of encryption technology in Australia and launched a series of strategic and investment measures to support the development (AustCyber, 2019). Meanwhile, the growth of global cybersecurity threats and stricter data security regulations have also promoted the rapid rise in the demand for encryption technology and professionals in various industries (Technology Decisions, 2025). However, compared with the United States, Europe and some Asian countries, Australia's overall influence in cryptography research and practical usage is still relatively limited, and it faces some challenges and gaps in basic innovation and large-scale application.

In contrast to systems like Microsoft 365, which require seamless integration of cryptographic tools, large-scale infrastructure, and high-assurance security policies, Australia's current cryptography industry remains limited in its ability to independently deliver such systems. The gap is not just in technology, but also in workforce scale, research depth, and industry maturity. These limitations constrain Australia's capacity to build sovereign, secure platforms at scale.

First, in terms of talent supply and demand, Australia faces a significant talent shortage. The demand for talent in the field of cybersecurity has exploded in recent years, and encryption technology experts are in short supply. About 17,000 additional cyber security professionals will be needed across the country by 2026 to meet demand according to Australia's cyber security partnership AustCyber (AustCyber, 2019).

The shortage has driven up salaries, with positions such as "cryptographer" earning far more on average than typical IT jobs, reflecting the importance of encryption to the digital economy and national security as well as the scarcity of specialist talent (CampusReview, 2022; SalaryExpert, 2025). Employers also have a relatively high demand for cryptography-related positions, usually expecting candidates to have a high degree (master's or doctoral degree) background and solid theoretical and practical skills in cryptography. These factors show that Australia still has a lot of room for improvement in cultivating and attracting cryptographic talents.

[Original figure omitted from the text edition.]

Secondly, in terms of technical products and independent innovation, Australia lacks local encryption solutions and relies on foreign mature technologies to a considerable extent. The encryption algorithms and security products adopted by many Australian government agencies and enterprises are mainly from international general solutions. For example, AES, RSA and other widely used algorithms are invented by overseas experts and become standards, while Australia has no independent design of cryptographic algorithms that are widely adopted as international mainstream standards. At the same time, there are relatively few local encryption products with global influence. There are a small number of Australian cybersecurity companies working on encryption products, and one of the few notable examples is Senetas, which focuses on high- performance network encryption devices. The company's high-speed encryptors have been certified by the new Australian government and adopted by dozens of governments and institutions around the world, and its products are the first to integrate newly standardized quantum-resistant encryption algorithms to provide quantum security protection. But apart from these and other cases, most of the solutions provided by local Australian manufacturers still integrate encryption libraries and hardware developed abroad, rather than inventing new algorithms or systems. The small size of the local crypto industry also leads to a lack of business incentives to drive crypto innovation entirely domestically. This structural imbalance is further illustrated in comparative economic data. As shown in Figure 8, Australia's cybersecurity industry significantly over-indexes in services across all security layers, while severely under-indexing in hardware and software. This suggests that local providers are primarily engaged in service integration and support, rather than developing foundational cryptographic technologies or systems. As a result, Australia remains heavily reliant on imported products and external innovation.

This imbalance reflects a fatal vulnerability in Australia’s cybersecurity capability: while services are essential, long-term resilience depends on deeper local innovation in hardware and software cryptography.

Consequently, Australia largely dependent on global cryptography research advances and imported products to meet its needs. Corporate users are relatively cautious and conservative in adopting cryptographic technologies. For example, although a survey showed that 61% of Australian organizations have recognized the threat of quantum computing to existing encryption, only about 8.5% of the surveyed organizations have started to deploy quantum-resistant cryptographic solutions, (Technology Decisions, 2025).It can be seen that in terms of the application of emerging encryption technologies, Australian enterprises are still in a wait-and-see state and slow in technological innovation.

At the same time, Australia is also facing challenges in improving the scientific research strength and industrial scale of cryptography. Cryptography research requires highly specialized talents and long-term investment. Compared with the large research groups and huge R&D investment in North America and Europe, the size and research funding of the relevant research community in Australia are limited, resulting in relatively few cutting-edge results. To some extent, this lack of investment limits Australia's voice in international cryptographic standards and innovations. However, it is worth noting that Australia has shown some bright results in the field of quantum-secure encryption (PQC) in recent years. A team led by Monash University and participating in the Data61 division of the federal research agency CSIRO has developed an efficient quantum-resistant encryption algorithm "LaV", which aims to improve the security of end-to-end encryption under the threat of quantum computing. It is reported that the algorithm is considered to be one of the most efficient quantum-resistant encryption methods at present and has been released at the top international cryptography conference Crypto 2023 (Monash University, 2023; Live Science, 2023), which has won the attention and recognition of the academic community. This result shows that Australian researchers are working on the frontier of cryptography and contributing to the emerging security challenges brought about by quantum computing. In terms of international cooperation, Australia has actively engaged with the NIST post-quantum cryptography standardization project. Its national agencies monitor and contribute to candidate evaluation, and researchers are encouraged to participate in NIST’s submission and testing rounds through domestic trials and analysis (Cyber.gov.au, 2023). All these show that Australia, despite its small size, is trying to maintain a place in the development of cryptography technology by focusing on specific directions and international collaboration.

Overall, the current situation of the Australian cryptography industry presents both opportunities and challenges. On the one hand, there are remarkable local achievements in emerging fields such as post-quantum cryptography, and this might give Australia a competitive edge, allowing it to achieve a significant lead in quantum cryptography in the future. Furthermore, some security vendors have achieved international recognition, and local experts are contributing to the progress of global cryptography technology. On the other hand, there is still a significant gap between Australia and the international leading level in terms of basic innovation capability, industrial scale and strategic importance. To fill this gap, Australia needs to make sustained efforts in various aspects: This includes increasing long-term investment in cryptography research, educating and attracting more local crypto talent, encouraging the growth of a local cybersecurity industry. For example, through government procurement and research funding to create market opportunities for local products. Afterwards, Australia should create policies that treat strong encryption as a tool for digital security, not a barrier to law enforcement. This will help the country stay competitive in future cryptography development. By this way can Australia be better positioned to compete in the cryptographic technology in the future.

Conclusion

In this project, we explored the encryption architecture of Microsoft 365, illustrating how it effectively integrates advanced encryption technologies, robust identity management, and user-friendly policy controls. Microsoft 365’s strength lies not just in powerful cryptographic algorithms but also in a reliable infrastructure and clear governance practices. The platform successfully applies the Extended CIA Triad principles—confidentiality through multi-layered encryption, integrity and authenticity via digital signatures and identity verification, and availability ensured by distributed key management and redundancy.

Additionally, Microsoft’s system underscores the importance of balancing security with usability, highlighting that encryption is most effective when supported by comprehensive identity management, policy enforcement, and accessible design choices. This also appeals that a high-level ability and resources are needed to run this complex system locally.

By contrast, Australia's cryptography industry is still maturing and faces significant hurdles such as talent shortages, limited local research capacity, and reliance on imported technologies. However, Australia has begun taking strategic steps to close this gap. Notably, the national quantum strategy and increased investments in post-quantum cryptography reflect a growing recognition of encryption’s vital role. Legislative adjustments are also underway to better align cybersecurity with law enforcement needs.

Despite facing with these challenges, Australian researchers are already contributing to global innovation, particularly in quantum-safe encryption. By focusing on these emerging areas, Australia has an opportunity to develop a stronger local industry and shift from being predominantly a consumer to becoming a meaningful contributor in the global cryptographic landscape. Ultimately, fostering this shift will require ongoing investment, strengthened education initiatives, and active international collaboration.
